## Educational purpose

Use the two independently varying quantities—number of equal groups and objects per group—to determine and justify a product. This focuses the equal-groups structural relationship rather than repeating the existing lesson's focus on translating equal additions into multiplication notation.

## Target audience

Children 6–8 who can count collections to 25 and tell whether groups contain the same number. English is editorial source, not an implementation locale; every learner-facing phrase will need genuine supported-language translation.

## Prerequisites

- Count objects accurately up to 25, including groups of three to five.
- Distinguish the number of groups from the number in each group; repeated addition is helpful but not mandatory.
- Recognize a multiplication sign with support; no memorized facts required.

## Expected learning outcomes

- Identify whether groups are equal in size and state group count and group size separately.
- Find a total by counting G groups of S objects, explaining G × S = total under the declared groups-first convention.
- Explain why an unequal arrangement cannot directly use one count × one common group size without regrouping.
- Vary group count while holding group size fixed, and vice versa, and predict how the total changes.

## Essential subject matter

For G equally sized groups each with S objects, the total is G × S. Examples: 3 groups of 4 = 12; 4 groups of 3 = 12; 2 groups of 5 = 10. An unequal picture with group sizes [4,3,4] has total 11 but cannot accurately be described as three groups of four. Group roles are chosen by the narrated model, not fixed globally by the position of factors. All instructional totals ≤25.

## Conceptual progression and dependencies

Recognize equal group sizes → count whole group totals → manipulate one structural variable at a time → reject an unequal-group model → build a new model and explain the product.

## Misconceptions, examples and boundaries

Adding G+S instead of G×S (3+4=7 instead of twelve); confusing number of groups with counters in each; counting only one group; interpreting [4,3,4] as [4,4,4] without changing the model. Keep nonnegative whole numbers and no division, formal arrays or times-table speed drills.
