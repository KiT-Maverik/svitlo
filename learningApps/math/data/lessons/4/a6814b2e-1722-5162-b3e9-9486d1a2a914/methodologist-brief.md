## Educational purpose

Introduce an unknown addend as a missing part of a known whole and connect part–whole reasoning with subtraction as the inverse of addition. The learning goal is to find a missing quantity while preserving equality, not to memorize a blank-filling trick.

## Target audience

Children aged 6–8 in grades 1–2 who are learning to read equations and solve addition/subtraction situations. Assume uneven literacy; pair a short written equality with a spoken description and a concrete part–whole model. Avoid requiring verbal explanation as the only way to show understanding.

## Prerequisites

- Count and compare quantities within the selected range and understand a total as made from parts.
- Add and subtract small nonnegative whole numbers, with objects or a number line as needed.
- Interpret an equals sign as a relationship between two equal quantities, not merely a signal to write an answer.

## Expected learning outcomes

- Identify which part of an addition relation is unknown in both □ + 5 = 12 and 5 + □ = 12.
- Explain that the whole is the total and removing the known part leaves the missing part.
- Find the missing addend using subtraction in an age-appropriate number domain (initially totals up to 20), with an optional model.
- Check the proposed value by substituting it into the **original addition equality**, and notice when the resulting sum is wrong.

## Essential subject matter

An **addend** is a quantity being added; the **sum** is the total. In 5 + □ = 12, the missing addend is 7 because 12 − 5 = 7 and 5 + 7 = 12. Likewise, □ + 5 = 12 has the same unknown value. Part–whole representations make the inverse relationship visible: a whole of twelve counters, five known, leaves seven unknown. Subtraction works here because the sum and known addend are given and the task is within nonnegative whole numbers. The equality has the same meaning with the unknown on the left or right and with a placeholder or a verbal description. Future recall should anchor “whole minus known part gives missing part” together with the obligation to check it.

## Conceptual progression and dependencies

Connect physical part–whole models to written equalities. Show how a complete known addition can be decomposed into its parts before introducing a missing component. Relate removing the known part from the whole to subtraction, then verify by restoring the parts and comparing with the whole. Gradually shift to equations without objects and to contextual stories where the missing part occurs in different positions. The Screenwriter chooses the learner actions and pacing.

## Misconceptions, examples and boundaries

Common confusions: taking the known addend **away from itself**; adding the visible addend to the total; believing the blank must always be after the plus sign; treating the equals sign as “calculate what follows”; or checking only the subtraction rather than the original addition. Counterexample: if 5 + □ = 12, choosing 17 by adding 5 + 12 fails substitution. Include sums within 20 and no negative unknowns, fractions, or multi-step symbolic equation solving.

## Localization, accessibility and offline intent

Translate “addend”, “sum”, “whole”, “part”, and “equals” carefully and do not substitute culturally specific objects that distort quantity. Ensure equation reading remains correct for both LTR and RTL presentations; keep mathematical operator order semantically stable. Support counting by audio/text, tactile-friendly physical substitutes, keyboard-accessible input/selection, screen-reader description of missing boxes, adjustable text size and number line descriptions. Offline resources and feedback must be complete once downloaded; do not assume an online checker.
