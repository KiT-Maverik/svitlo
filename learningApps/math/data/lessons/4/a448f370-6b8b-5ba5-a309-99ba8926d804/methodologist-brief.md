## Educational purpose

Replace an unreliable visual guess ('larger-looking means heavier') with a **fair equal-arm balance comparison**. Children learn that the lower pan holds the greater mass, equal pan height indicates equal masses under comparable conditions, and physical size or container appearance alone cannot determine which mass is greater.

## Target audience

**Grade 1 (approximately ages 6–7).** Grade 1 (about ages 6–7) learners with intuitive heavy/light language, optional counting to five, early reading or nonvisual and motor access needs. A labeled symbolic pan-height and text-ledger alternative is required; no real weights, kitchen scales, physical lifting, precise timing or adult assistance may be assumed.

## Prerequisites

Understand words **more/less/equal** through everyday sets and comparisons, recognize two labeled objects A and B, follow a single controlled 'place one object on each side' observation. No standard grams/kilograms, counting of mass units, lever theory or prior reading of a numerical scale needed.

## Expected learning outcomes

- Use an equal-arm balance with one object on each pan to identify which has **greater mass**, which has less, or whether their masses are equal.
- Explain that the **lower pan** indicates the heavier object's side on an ideal fair balance, while level pans show equal mass (with the equipment set up comparably).
- Reject apparent volume, height, shape or color as sufficient evidence of which object is more massive when two unknown objects look very different.
- Recognize an **unfair comparison** (an extra object already on one pan or different balance setup) and restore fair equal conditions before interpreting.

## Essential subject matter

**Mass** concerns how much matter an object contains and is operationally compared by an ideal **equal-arm balance** under the same gravitational conditions. If a pair of unequal masses is placed on the two equal arms, the **heavier side goes down**, and the lighter side rises; if masses are equal, the beam stays level (assuming a calibrated, unburdened balance). A larger-looking item **may** have smaller mass than a smaller dense item. For a deterministic teaching simulation, label two abstract object masses internally (e.g. small metal token mass 5 model units versus large foam block mass 2; equal pair 3 and 3). Do **not** introduce numerical grams or claim the token values are calibrated weight. No scale reading or unit selection is part of this Skill. Control for tare: compare **one** selected object on each pan, with identical pan hardware and no hidden add-ons; otherwise the display may be unfair. The difference between weight as a force and mass as a quantity is outside Grade 1 treatment.

## Conceptual progression and dependencies

Begin with a large light-looking foam block and small dense-looking token, ask for prediction before showing any result. Place them on a fair equal-arm balance and observe the **small token's side lowering**. In an adjacent contrast, two differently shaped objects produce level pans when their masses are equal. Allow learners to swap sides to check the result reverses pan orientation but not which object is heavier; deliberately show an extra object on one pan and ask how to make the comparison fair. Transfer to an unfamiliar pair with reasoning from balanced state, not appearance alone.

## Misconceptions, examples and boundaries

Bigger object always has more mass; higher pan is heavier because it is 'up'; a tilted scale means a larger-looking object won; color signals heaviness; identical-looking boxes must have equal mass; two unequal arm lengths can be interpreted as an equal-arm balance; placing a hidden extra object on one side produces a valid comparison. Weight versus mass words need age-appropriate translation without asserting physical equivalence in all contexts.

**Depth exclusions:** Only comparative mass on a schematic calibrated **equal-arm** balance, one object per pan, and equality. No grams/kilograms, digital scale readings, balancing three masses with arithmetic, buoyancy, density computation, torque equations, gravity differences, or real heavy-object handling. The qualitative balance behavior is a pedagogical ideal model, not a claim about particular household hardware. These boundaries govern a **Lesson**, not the grade-independent canonical Skill definition.
