## Educational purpose

Teach qualitative magnitude and contextual checks as a distinct verification habit, even before or independently of exact recomputation.

## Target audience

**Primary audience: Grade 3.** Use short clear instructions, optional audio read-aloud, large touch targets and a nonvisual text representation. This is an instructional depth decision for the Lesson, not a grade restriction on the canonical Skill.

## Prerequisites

- Read and interpret simple quantitative stories; estimate sums using tens/hundreds; compare three-digit numbers; understand what a count can and cannot represent.

## Target Skills

- [checkReasonableness.data.ts](../../../../data/skills/reasoning/verification/skills/checkReasonableness.data.ts)

## Expected learning outcomes

- Reject answers incompatible with context or plausible magnitude; estimate a reasonable range before exact calculation; explain why 98 + 105 cannot plausibly equal 2,000; identify when a nonnegative count or unchanged units matters.

## Essential subject matter

Two groups of roughly 100 objects total roughly 200, not 2,000; 98 + 105 = 203. Estimates are approximate checks, not exact proof. Counting objects cannot produce a negative quantity in simple collection contexts. An estimate can fail to catch a small computation error, so contextual plausibility is not equivalent to verification by an independent exact method.

## Conceptual progression and dependencies

Begin with a stark magnitude contradiction, make the rough benchmark explicit, check quantities and units, then transfer independently to a novel realistic claim.

## Misconceptions, examples and boundaries

'Looks close' is not sufficient explanation. Rounding 98 to 100 and 105 to 100 suggests about 200, but 200 is not the exact answer. Do not infer that an answer close to estimate is necessarily correct. Exclude advanced statistical and dimensional-analysis arguments.
