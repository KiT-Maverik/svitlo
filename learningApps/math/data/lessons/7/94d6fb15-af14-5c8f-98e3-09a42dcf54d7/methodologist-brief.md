## Educational purpose

Teach how a missing factor in a multiplication can be reconstructed by dividing the known product by the known **nonzero factor**, then verified in the original multiplication. The representation should retain the equal-groups meaning and distinguish “number of groups” from “number in each” according to a stated example convention. One selected number is not proof of mastered multiplication or division.

## Target audience

Readiness-dependent enrichment for children aged approximately 6–8 who **already understand** equal groups, multiplication expressions, and simple exact division. Not a core expectation of all grade 1–2 learners. Use concrete models and short wording before terms factor/product; offer accessible alternatives to dragging or speech.

## Prerequisites

- Read 3 × 4 = 12 as three groups of four under the currently adopted group-count × group-size convention.
- Count equal groups, identify a product, and use division as exact equal sharing or grouping.
- Understand an unknown □ as a missing value and the equals sign as a true relationship.
- Know that the supplied factor is **nonzero** and that candidate numbers must be checked with multiplication.

## Expected learning outcomes

- Identify missing factor (not product) in 3 × □ = 12 and □ × 4 = 12.
- Use the product and known nonzero factor to compute the missing factor: 12 ÷ 3 = 4 or 12 ÷ 4 = 3.
- Relate both factor positions to appropriately labeled equal-groups stories, without swapping group roles silently.
- Verify the answer in original multiplication (3 × 4 = 12; 3 × 4 = 12 with the corresponding position).
- Transfer to 4 × □ = 20, with accessible model support on request.

## Essential subject matter

For whole numbers, a known product P and nonzero known factor k yield the missing factor m = P ÷ k **when division is exact in the chosen number domain**. For 3 × □ = 12, think three equal groups making twelve; each group has four, and 12 ÷ 3 = 4. For □ × 4 = 12, consider groups of four: three groups make twelve, and 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Under the example convention (groups × size), position changes the modeled role, not the multiplication product's numerical value. Restore the candidate to the original equality to verify. If the known factor were zero and product zero, its partner would not be uniquely determined; this Lesson excludes that case and does not teach general symbolic equations.

## Conceptual progression and dependencies

Activate a complete known multiplication model, then hide one input (either number of groups or size). With a fixed product and known factor, reveal the missing count through equal sharing/grouping. Introduce division as the inverse procedure; perform the original multiplication as a check. Contrast with wrongly adding k+P or using k÷P. Add an independent example only after model roles are clear.

## Misconceptions, examples and boundaries

Children may confuse factor and product, calculate 3+12=15 or 3÷12, forget the group convention, or assume changing factor order leaves the picture identical without reinterpreting it. For 3×□=12 choose □=4; inserting 5 yields 15, not 12. For □×4=12 the blank is 3 groups, not 4 items. Factors 1–5 and totals ≤25; exact nonnegative whole-number division, no non-exact factors, negative numbers, fractions, zero as supplied factor or division algorithms.

## Localization, accessibility and offline intent

Translate “factor”, “product”, “equal groups”, “group size” and “how many groups” in natural child-friendly language. All arrangements described with precise group count and each size; RTL must preserve operand order/meaning. Keyboard, screen reader and touch-equivalent counting/verification, low-device simple models and full offline data/feedback, without external media or speech recognition.
