## Educational purpose

Assign observations to unambiguous categories, count the frequency of each category, and check that the category counts add to the total. The intended conceptual change is from judging by appearance or one tempting example to checking the specific mathematical condition. This lesson serves one independently assessable canonical Skill without altering its identity.

## Target audience

**Primary grade: Grade 1**, approximately ages 6–7. Counting disjoint categories with small totals extends one-to-one counting without needing graph literacy or formal statistics vocabulary. Assume mixed literacy and language fluency: child-facing phrasing must be short and supported by visible and nonvisual equivalents.

## Prerequisites

- Count reliably from 1 to 12.
- Recognize simple circle, triangle and square outlines.
- Match one observed item to one stated category.

Prerequisite competence is a planning assumption, not evidence of mastery. A learner lacking it needs a separate scaffold, not a new definition of the target Skill.

## Expected learning outcomes

- Use a single explicit classification rule for each observation.
- Count each of eight shapes exactly once into one of three categories.
- Give separate counts for four circles, three triangles, and one square.
- Check that 4 + 3 + 1 equals the eight original observations.
- Repeat the method independently with a different nine-object collection.

Completing an exploratory interaction is not independent validation of mastery. Any later formal evidence or scoring belongs to an assessment design.

## Essential subject matter and instructional depth

Categorical frequencies for 6–12 visible discrete observations and 2–3 nonoverlapping exhaustive categories. No charting, percentages, averages or inferred relationships.

Canonical collectCounts Skill defines frequency as number assigned to a category; exhaustive nonoverlapping category frequencies must sum to the total.

## Conceptual progression and dependencies

Start from the listed prerequisites, identify what information determines the answer, make a prediction, reveal a controlling comparison, and explain it before transfer. Treat a fixed collection of eight shapes as observations. Sorting by shape is deliberate, with no ambiguous hybrid shapes. A running tally makes missing or double-counted objects immediately visible. Conceptual depth is set for this lesson's grade only; the Skill taxonomy remains grade-independent.

## Misconceptions, examples and boundaries

- Count the same observation twice.
- A category count represents the position of a shape in a row, not how many were seen.
- Counts do not need to add back to the original total.
- Different-looking orientations of the same shape become different categories.

Concrete boundaries: Categorical frequencies for 6–12 visible discrete observations and 2–3 nonoverlapping exhaustive categories. No charting, percentages, averages or inferred relationships. Repeated exposure alone must not be presented as mastery.
