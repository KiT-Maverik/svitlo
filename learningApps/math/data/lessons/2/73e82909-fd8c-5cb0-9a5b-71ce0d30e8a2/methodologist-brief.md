## Educational purpose

Demonstrate that exchanging multiplication factors preserves the product, first by reorienting the SAME rectangular array of dots, then by independently checking a different pair of factors. The number of groups and the size of each group may change roles; the total does not.

## Target audience

Children aged 6–8 who can count rectangular arrays and read small multiplication statements; readiness support with exact dot counts. This is an autonomous Lesson and does not presume particular Program or class placement.

## Prerequisites

- Identify row and column count in a regular rectangular array.
- Know that R rows with C dots each contain R×C dots.
- Read the multiplication expression with factors and product, without requiring multiplication-table fluency.

## Expected learning outcomes

- Observe that one physical 3-by-4 array can be reoriented to 4-by-3 without adding or removing dots.
- Explain in words and numerals why 3×4 and 4×3 both equal 12.
- Predict that changing factor order in 2×5 yields the same product as 5×2 and verify by counting.
- Distinguish swapping factors from changing either factor's value; avoid claiming subtraction is commutative.

## Essential subject matter

Commutativity: a×b=b×a in the relevant whole-number domain. The same twelve-dot rectangle can be viewed as 3 rows of 4 or 4 rows of 3; a quarter turn changes orientation, not cardinality. 2×5=10 and 5×2=10 likewise count the same ten dots arranged as 2 rows of 5 or 5 rows of 2. Rotating or reorganizing must preserve each dot exactly once, not silently copy objects. The factor roles in a 'groups × size' narrative must be relabeled when the orientation changes.

## Conceptual progression and dependencies

Recall a countable 3-by-4 array → rotate the same twelve objects and name new rows/columns → predict the relation a×b=b×a → transfer to 2×5 and its reversal.

## Misconceptions, examples and boundaries

Mistaking rotation for doubling; interpreting 3×4 and 4×3 as two different totals; assuming any operation is commutative (e.g., 5−2=2−5); treating a rearranged 'four groups of three' story as the original unchanged 'three groups of four' story. Numbers 0–5 and products ≤25 in models; no algebraic proof requirement.
