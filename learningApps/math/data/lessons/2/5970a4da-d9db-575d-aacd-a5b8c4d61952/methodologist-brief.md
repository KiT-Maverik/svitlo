## Educational purpose

Establish three meanings of subtraction: removing part of a collection, comparing two quantities to find their difference, and finding an unknown part of a known whole. These are different situations represented by the same arithmetic relationship, not three separate new Skills.

## Target audience

Children aged 6–8, grades 1–2. Early counters and addition within 20; short spoken/printed prompts, manipulatives and accessible equivalents for mixed reading fluency.

## Prerequisites

- Count and compare small nonnegative whole-number collections up to 20.
- Understand parts making a whole and be able to count what remains or what is missing.
- Recognize subtraction and equals signs with support; no written algorithm or column subtraction required.

## Expected learning outcomes

- Identify whether a story asks what remains after removal, how much more one quantity has than another, or which part of a known whole is missing.
- Demonstrate each situation with countable objects or equivalent numerical descriptions.
- Represent all three contexts using an accurate subtraction equality within 20, naming what the numbers mean.
- Explain why the same subtraction equation can answer different story questions, while keeping each story's known and unknown roles clear.

## Essential subject matter

Removing: 9 counters, remove 4, 5 remain, so 9 − 4 = 5. Comparing: 9 objects in one group and 4 in another, five **more** in the first, so 9 − 4 = 5. Missing part: 9 altogether, 4 are known, the missing part is 5, so 9 − 4 = 5. A subtraction is not always literal physical removal: comparison may leave both collections intact. Negative answers, formal unknown-variable notation, long multi-step stories and subtraction algorithms are outside scope.

## Conceptual progression and dependencies

Start with familiar “take away” and visible counters. Preserve the same values to contrast comparison and missing-part roles without forcing a new computation. Invite selection of the story type, representation, equality and explanatory answer. Move from counters to an independent short word problem.

## Misconceptions, examples and boundaries

Children may remove four in the comparison story instead of keeping both groups; compare by combining 9 and 4; subtract the wrong way around; mistake “missing” as physically lost; or assume every subtraction always means taking objects away. Explicitly contrast asking “How many remain?” with “How many more?” Use nonnegative whole numbers with minuends no larger than 20.

## Localization, accessibility and offline intent

Translate “left”, “more than”, “difference”, “missing part”, “whole”, “take away” distinctly; do not depend on physical left/right placement, culture-specific stories, colored counters, narration or dragging. Present equivalent structured descriptions of both groups, keyboard controls, RTL-safe number order and local offline feedback.
